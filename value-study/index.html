Tonal organization

Advanced guided workspace · same photo and crop

The computer measures brightness. You decide which shapes should belong together. Work from a blurred reference, choose a light/dark family, then experiment with intentional grouping and contrast. These are artistic studies, not automatically correct solutions.

1 · Broad relationships (blur)

See the major transitions before forcing the photograph into two values.

2 · Your edited light / dark design

Click or drag to assign selected shapes to a different family. Corrections also carry into the 3–9-value studies.

Paint directly on the right-hand image. The original photograph is never changed.
Worked example · classical head (click to expand or collapse)
  1. Observe: the hair, illuminated forehead, shaded cheek, and background do not necessarily follow one easy binary split.
  2. Compare: look at the blurred grayscale image on the left and the two-value map on the right.
  3. Choose: try More light area, then move the cutoff yourself. Ask whether the broad facial planes read more clearly.
  4. Correct: paint a shape into the light or dark family if the automatic cutoff places it badly. The goal is a deliberate, readable design.
  5. Develop: return to Value studies to subdivide the families, then test palette groupings below. Compare what is gained and lost.

Original instructional steps; informed by the concepts of major key, minor key and tonal grouping in the New Masters Academy Week 6 handout. No handout artwork is reproduced here.

3 · Design a restricted value palette

Start from a nine-step scale (1 = white, 9 = black). Select steps, join adjacent ones into one family, or omit them. Omitted tones are reassigned to the nearest remaining family; no pixels disappear.

Minor key · contrast range

Restricted-palette interpretation

All tonal changes are calculated from the same photograph. This palette design is separate from the locked, progressively subdivided value studies above.

Why the two exercises differ

Progressive subdivision preserves a decision about the existing light/dark boundary, then adds detail inside each family. Tonal organization deliberately restricts or combines values to improve clarity, emphasize a subject, or change major/minor key. The nine-step palette remaps brightness globally; painting corrections adds local artistic decisions. Neither process identifies actual physical shadows by itself.

Drawing guidance: Treat these as initial maps, not perfect artistic answer keys. Automatic cutoffs group photographed brightness, not the physical cause of that brightness. Local color (dark wood or hair) and cast/form shadow can end up in the same value group. If a boundary looks wrong, change the crop or click a different light/dark balance; higher value counts preserve all earlier divisions for that same crop and balance. Keep the manually drawn thumbnail small, flat, and simple; add gradients only in the later shaded drawing. All processing of uploaded photos stays on your device.
